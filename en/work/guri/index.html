Institutional ERP

GURI — Gestão Unificada de Recursos Institucionais

Modular ERP that manages the administrative resources of Universidade Federal do Pampa, with over 50 modules covering distinct areas of the institution.

Period
2012 — 2022
Status
In production
  • PHP
  • CodeIgniter
  • IBM DB2

Context

GURI is the institutional ERP of Universidade Federal do Pampa. The project documentation records development between 2012 and 2022, with the purpose of managing the administrative resources of the higher education institution across its most diverse areas, spread over more than 50 modules.

The software is not publicly available: it holds a computer program registration, which is what the university news item documents.

The problem

Running a higher education institution means serving very distinct areas at the same time: library, assets, purchasing, billing, ombudsman, institutional events, protocol, security, password recovery. These are different processes, with different data and different users.

Scale adds to it. There are more than 50 modules, which cannot be delivered as a single release — neither in schedule nor in the order demands arrive. A modular design is what lets each area move at its own pace, and the system has to sustain that modularity across ten years of use, not just the first release.

This study records what the project documentation proves: the purpose of the system, the technologies, and the role played in each module. What existed before, the criteria that guided the architecture and the technical decisions behind each module are not recorded and, therefore, are not asserted here.

The solution

GURI was built on PHP with the CodeIgniter framework, using IBM DB2 as the database. Modularity is the backbone of the system: the documented modules are independent units, each with its own purpose and its own responsibility role. Some had module management and requirements analysis; others were owned by a single feature.

Coverage runs from the institutional core — Portal, Security and Internal Audit — to very specific routines such as Library, Assets, Purchasing and Billing. There are also outward integrations: importing candidates for vacancies coming from SISU, password recovery and email alias creation, and communication with terminals that authenticate by fingerprint at the university restaurants.

Two module groups reveal design decisions worth pointing out. The Portal module concentrates login and action auditing, while the Security module controls internal permissions and audits user actions — the two responsibilities have walked together since the foundation. And next to Reports there is Report Dashboards, whose stated purpose is to let analysts register dashboards viewed on a client site, with no development involvement.

  • Internal Audit — control the institution's audit processes (Module Manager, Requirements Analyst)
  • Library — control book loans and catalog card issuance (Feature Developer)
  • Catalog Card Issuance — issue catalog cards from registered loans (Feature Developer)
  • Billing — generate payment slips for fees (Module Manager)
  • Purchasing — manage purchase requests from the departments (Developer)
  • Institutional Events — manage participant registrations, article submissions, evaluation processes and proceedings generation (Requirements Analyst, Lead Developer, Module Manager)
  • Scientific Production Extractor — retrieve scientific production data from an external source, compile it and generate researcher production reports (Requirements Analyst, Developer)
  • Newsletter — create electronic correspondence to be sent to user mailing lists (Analyst, Developer)
  • Ombudsman — receive reports from users and the community and manage their handling (Module Manager, Requirements Analyst)
  • Report Dashboards — register report dashboards for viewing on a client site (Requirements Analyst, Lead Developer)
  • Assets — manage institutional assets through registration, transfers, loans and inventory maintenance (Analyst and Feature Developer for Inventory)
  • Portal — main module, with login and action auditing (Requirements Analyst, Developer)
  • Protocol — record and control the internal flow of documentation (Requirements Analyst, Developer)
  • Reports — create and issue standardized or custom reports, produced by analysts and made available to users (Requirements Analyst, Developer)
  • University Restaurant — control information and state of the university restaurants, including terminal communication and fingerprint authentication (Developer)
  • Security — control internal system permissions and audit user actions (Analyst, Developer)
  • SISU — allow importing data on candidates for vacancies and ease the enrollment process (Requirements Analyst, Developer)
  • Password Recovery Support — manage password recovery requests and email alias creation (Lead Developer, Frontend)
  • External Users — allow registering accounts for external users, except students and employees with a direct bond (Requirements Analyst, Developer, Documenter)

My role

I worked on GURI in five distinct roles: Requirements Analyst, Module Manager, Developer, Documenter, and Code and Documentation Reviewer. No module was merely delivered — the responsibility ran from gathering the need to reviewing what was already in production.

The technical coordination record overlaps with GURI's. Between January 2016 and July 2019 I coordinated the development teams, a period when the ERP was in full development. Defining processes, standards and criteria for code and documentation review was not a theoretical exercise: it was what held up a system with more than 50 modules and over a decade of maintenance.

The concentration of roles shows the shape of the team, not a succession of job titles: part of the work was understanding the domain well enough to specify it, part was implementing it, and part was making sure what already existed stayed consistent.

Limits of this study

The numbers below are structural — they count modules, years and roles. There is no operational impact metric in the material consulted: nothing records how many users the system has, how much time a process saves, or what prior state it replaced.

There is also no public code or environment to demonstrate. That is why this study carries no screenshot and no implementation example, and why any claim about performance, adoption or return on investment would be invented.
